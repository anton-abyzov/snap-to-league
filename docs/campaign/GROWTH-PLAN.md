# Snap to League growth plan

Prepared September 27, 2026. This is a proposed campaign and product backlog. No paid ads, outreach messages, tracking changes or SEO changes have been launched by this document.

Start with organizers who already collect results on paper or in screenshots. The first goal is to get one organizer through a complete, observed workflow with their own permitted sample, then learn whether they return for the next event. Views and hackathon attention are useful sources of introductions; they are not adoption evidence.

[Try Snap](https://snap.easychamp.com) · [Current 110-second demonstration](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-ZdivkQQo9k) · [Submitted Devpost project](https://devpost.com/software/snap-to-league) · [Source](https://github.com/anton-abyzov/snap-to-league)

## What we can promise

**Photograph results. Review the draft. Share a competition people can follow.**

Snap imports photos or screenshots, lets the organizer review names and scores, computes standings from match results, and compares another photo with the current draft. Supported game competitions publish through EasyChamp after sign-in. Placement contests, including trivia and cup stacking, share a board on Snap. Keep those two publishing paths explicit.

The demonstration contains real recorded interactions and labeled samples. It shows comparison against an unsaved draft, not proof of a new saved EasyChamp update. EasyChamp identity, competition publishing and league pages existed before ShellHacks; the event work added Snap's import, review, reconciliation and integration. Avoid accuracy percentages, guaranteed time savings, adoption claims, winning claims and sponsor endorsement. See [EVIDENCE-AND-CLAIMS.md](EVIDENCE-AND-CLAIMS.md).

## First audiences and offer

| Priority | Organizer | Observable reason to contact | Offer | Evidence to collect |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Campus recreation staff and student tournament organizers | A public upcoming cup with results handled on paper, a whiteboard or screenshots | Walk through one redacted or synthetic scoresheet together | Their current steps, corrections they make, whether they can reach a reviewed result |
| 2 | Local amateur tournament and club administrators | They run repeated match days and share tables manually | Try one completed round, then the next round's photo | Name mismatches, results needing review, repeat use on a later day |
| 3 | Campus trivia, social games and activity organizers | They publish placements or a leaderboard | Build and share a Snap placement board | Whether a Snap board meets their needs; do not pitch EasyChamp placement publishing |

Use public event and organization pages for research. Record the evidence URL and date. Do not assume a club's tools, budget or pain from its name alone. Start with 15 researched organizations, five per segment; choose the first ten contacts with a real workflow match. These counts are proposed work targets, not performance forecasts.

The first offer is a small product-feedback session, not a purchase commitment or a claim that implementation is already included. Let the organizer use a sample with names removed. Do not ask strangers to email raw participant lists or private photos.

## Sequence for the next four weeks

In week one, restore reliable demo availability, record the supported workflow and select the first ten researched contacts. Release the distinct founder and EasyChamp cuts with different captions and verified destinations. The founder cut explains the problem and a real design choice; the EasyChamp cut shows the task an organizer can complete.

In week two, conduct the first feedback sessions. Ask the organizer to narrate their existing process, try their sample and explain what they would do next. Record failures and corrections alongside successful attempts. Produce one short tutorial that answers a question heard in those sessions.

In week three, invite organizers who completed a first trial to try a later round. Publish a worked example using synthetic or explicitly permitted results. Write one useful search page for the strongest observed use case, rather than several thin pages targeting adjacent keywords.

In week four, compare segments using observed completion and repeat use. Continue the segment that reaches a reviewed result and returns. If interest does not become a completed trial, fix the landing page or workflow before buying more visits. If no one returns, interview the initial users about frequency, publishing needs and trust; do not describe initial curiosity as product-market fit.

## Three paid-ad concepts to prepare

These are creative tests, not active ads. Begin only after the landing page works reliably and the review-completion event has been verified. Set an explicit test budget before launch. Compare one audience and one primary message at a time; do not call a winning ad from a handful of clicks.

### 1. The scoresheet is the starting point

**Audience:** campus and amateur tournament organizers. **Format:** 15–20-second vertical proof cut for Instagram/Facebook, adaptable to TikTok. **Hypothesis:** showing the existing paper workflow will attract more relevant organizers than a generic AI pitch.

Open on a readable permitted scoresheet: “Your results are already here.” Show selecting its photo, then the actual review screen: “Check names and scores.” End on the corresponding standings: “Turn a scoresheet into standings you can review.” Label synthetic inputs and previously published pages wherever applicable; do not imply the shown standings were just published if that was not recorded.

**Primary text:** “Start with a photo of your tournament results. Review the names and scores, then publish supported game competitions through EasyChamp. Try Snap to League.”

**Headline:** “Photo to reviewed standings” **CTA:** “Try the sample.” **Destination:** the working sample entry point on Snap. **Primary metric:** completed reviewed drafts per qualified landing visit. **Guardrail:** extraction failures and abandoned reviews.

### 2. Another photo. A clear review.

**Audience:** organizers running repeated rounds. **Format:** 20-second screen-led video for Meta or a concise LinkedIn demonstration. **Hypothesis:** a concrete update-review task will produce more repeat interest than the first-import novelty.

Show the first draft, a second photo and the actual difference view. The overlay says “Review what changed.” Show the organizer checking the changed result. End with “Keep the organizer in control.” Do not cut directly to a saved live page in a way that implies this unsaved demonstration published an update.

**Primary text:** “A new round means new results. Add another photo in Snap to League and review what changed before deciding what to publish.”

**Headline:** “Review the next round” **CTA:** “Watch the workflow.” **Destination:** a dedicated, working demo page with the recorded workflow and sample CTA. **Primary metric:** qualified visitors who complete a first review and later bring a second image. **Guardrail:** anyone who expects automatic, unchecked publishing.

### 3. Give the campus game a board

**Audience:** campus activity and trivia organizers. **Format:** 15-second vertical video or three-card carousel. **Hypothesis:** a placement example will reveal a separate campus use case without confusing it with league publishing.

Show a labeled sample trivia result sheet, its reviewed placements and a Snap shared board. Keep the words “Shared on Snap” visible at the payoff. Use event footage as atmosphere only; participants in the footage are not customers or endorsers.

**Primary text:** “Running trivia or a campus challenge? Start from the result photo, check the placements and share a board on Snap to League.”

**Headline:** “Give results a shared board” **CTA:** “See a sample board.” **Destination:** a curated public Snap example with synthetic results and a route back to the app. **Primary metric:** placement-board reviews followed by a successful share. **Guardrail:** requests for unsupported EasyChamp placement stages.

For all three, footage about AI does not itself mean the footage was generated by AI. Apply platform disclosures to the actual creative, including synthetic narration if used. Review badges, phone notifications, names and QR codes in the final encoded asset. Never use the event location or logos to imply university, organizer or sponsor endorsement.

## Measurement and decision rules

Instrumentation below is proposed. Its end-to-end delivery has not been verified. A UTM query string alone is not conversion tracking. Preserve campaign/cut/source on the first visit, use a pseudonymous session or authorized account identifier, and never include uploaded names, image content or scores in marketing analytics.

| Metric | Definition | Decision it supports |
|---|---|---|
| Qualified visit | Tagged landing session that opens the sample/demo or states an organizer use case | Whether a channel attracts the intended audience |
| Review completion | Unique draft reaches review and the organizer explicitly finishes checking it | Whether the core task works; mere extraction completion is insufficient |
| First-trial completion rate | Completed first reviews divided by first-trial starts; split real inputs from samples | Where users stop; report numerator and denominator |
| Outcome rate | Successful authorized game publication or Snap placement share divided by reviewed drafts seeking that outcome | Whether review leads to something useful; keep the two paths separate |
| Repeat organizer | An organizer completes a review on a later event/day, not a second tab of the same test | Whether the workflow recurs |
| Correction burden | Manual changes per completed draft, reported by field category and image type | Which extraction failures deserve product work |
| Outreach response | Positive replies and completed observed trials per delivered researched contact | Which audience/offer deserves another small batch |
| Acquisition cost | Paid spend divided by verified completed reviews; later by repeat organizers | Whether to continue a paid experiment; never substitute clicks for adoption |
| Availability and errors | Landing/app failures per checked attempt; extraction and publish failures separately | Whether traffic should wait for a reliability fix |

For the first cycle, target ten actual organizer conversations, three observed completed trials and two later repeat trials. These are internal learning goals, not predicted conversion rates or evidence of success. If fewer than three trials complete, fix the observed blockers before expanding outreach. If trials complete but none repeat, interview users before enlarging a paid test. Review weekly using counts, sample size and failure notes, not percentages without denominators.
